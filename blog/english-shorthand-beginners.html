How to start Pitman shorthand as a beginner

A practical roadmap for building readable outlines, dependable transcription and speed that lasts.

By Steno MasterPublished 26 September 20268-minute read

English Pitman shorthand is a sound-based writing system. Instead of spelling every word in full, learners represent sounds with strokes, positions and joins. The method becomes manageable when each rule is learned in sequence and tested through transcription. Trying to memorise hundreds of outlines at once usually creates confusion; a smaller daily routine produces steadier progress.

1. Build the right Pitman foundation

Start with consonant strokes and their correct direction, length and thickness. These features carry meaning, so careless stroke formation can make an outline difficult to read. Once the basic strokes are consistent, learn vowel placement and word position. Add common joins only after individual strokes feel natural.

Brief forms and common phrases can save time, but they work best after the underlying theory is clear. Write each new form several times, use it inside a sentence and read it back later. Recall after a gap is a better test than copying the same outline repeatedly.

Accuracy rule: if you cannot read an outline confidently after ten minutes, it is not yet ready for speed practice.

2. Use a four-part daily routine

  1. Theory review: spend 10 minutes revising one small rule or stroke group.
  2. Copy practice: write model outlines slowly and compare shape, position and joining.
  3. Dictation: take 10–15 minutes of audio at a comfortable speed without pausing every word.
  4. Transcription: type or write the passage in longhand, then classify every error.

A 35–45 minute routine completed consistently is more useful than one long session followed by several inactive days. Keep the same notebook, date every session and record the dictation speed and error count.

3. Follow a four-week beginner plan

Week 1: strokes and control

Practise the basic consonant strokes, correct direction and consistent size. Read every page back. Speed is not a target during this stage.

Week 2: vowels and position

Add vowel signs and practise placing outlines in the correct position. Use short word lists and simple sentences so each rule appears in context.

Week 3: joins and common forms

Study frequent joins, brief forms and a limited set of phrases. Mix old and new material to avoid recognising forms only in the order they were taught.

Week 4: controlled dictation

Begin short dictations at a speed where most outlines remain readable. Transcribe every session and repeat difficult passages after reviewing the relevant theory.

4. Review errors instead of merely counting them

Separate errors into four groups: theory errors, unreadable outlines, missed words and transcription mistakes. Each category needs a different response. A theory error requires revision; an unreadable outline requires cleaner formation; missed words may indicate that the audio is too fast; typing errors need separate keyboard practice.

Increase speed in small steps only after several sessions are accurate and readable. The goal is controlled improvement, not a single impressive recording. For structured learning, review the English shorthand course levels and use the Student Corner practice materials.

Common beginner questions

How long should a beginner practise every day?

Start with 30–45 focused minutes. Increase duration only when you can maintain concentration and still review the transcript carefully.

When should I begin speed dictation?

Begin controlled dictation after the basic strokes, vowels and common joins are readable. Use a comfortable speed and move upward gradually.

Is transcription necessary?

Yes. Transcription proves whether your notes communicate the original passage accurately and reveals mistakes that writing practice alone can hide.
